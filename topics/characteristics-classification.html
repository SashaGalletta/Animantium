Characteristics and Classification of Living Organisms

Topic 1

1.1

Biology is the study of life

Characteristics of living organisms

Biologists use a list of characteristics that living organisms show

  • Respiration
  • Sensitivity (irritability)
  • Nutrition
  • Growth and development
  • Excretion
  • Reproduction
  • Movement

“MRS GREN” is used to remember these.

How the characteristics of life depend on each other

Each of the characteristics of life is linked to the others.

Example: Organisms grow when they have access to a source of nutrients.

Growth and development

Growth is a permanent increase in size and dry mass. Development involves changes in an organism's form.

Excretion

The removal from organisms of toxic materials, the waste products of metabolism, and substances in excess of requirements.

Reproduction

The processes that produce new individuals of the same kind of organism. An organism may split into two, or reproduction may involve more complex processes, including fertilisation.

Complex organisation

Even the simplest living cell contains many different complex substances arranged in very specific structures.

Variations: Offspring

Offspring are different from one another and from their parents.

Ringer’s solution is a solution of ions and molecules that physiologists use to keep living tissues alive outside the body.

Depending on energy

The ability of an organism to carry out its life processes depends on the supply of energy.

Nutrition

The taking in of materials for energy, growth and development.

Example: Plants → light, carbon dioxide, water and ions to make their food.

Example: Animals → organic compounds, ions and water.

Respiration

The chemical reactions that break down nutrient molecules in living cells to release energy for metabolism.

Organisms have gas exchange systems that supply their cells with oxygen from their environment.

Sensitivity (irritability)

The ability to detect or sense changes in the internal or external environment and to make appropriate responses.

The changes are called stimuli.

1.2

The variety of life

The need to classify living things

Variation and natural selection lead to evolution. Evolution and the isolation of populations can lead to the development of new species. Every species has different characteristics, and some of these characteristics can be inherited by successive generations of the same species.

It is important in:

Conservation: Scientists need to be able to identify different organisms, and they need to control which organisms are used in breeding programmes.

Understanding evolutionary relationships: Classification helps scientists understand the relationships between organisms.

Five kingdoms

  • Protoctists
  • Plants
  • Fungi
  • Animals

Classification: The science of putting living organisms into categories.

Taxonomists: People who study classification.

Classification keys

Organisms can be identified by asking questions. These questions form a classification key.

Classification keys are based on morphology and anatomy.

Protein structure: Organisms that are closely related have very similar amino acid sequences in proteins such as haemoglobin.

DNA structure: Closely related organisms have very similar base sequences in their DNA because there has been less evolutionary time for mutations to accumulate.

New species may be classified based on characteristics such as protein structure, chromosome number or genes.

1.3

Fungi

Fungal cells have a common structure

Fungi are a very large group of organisms. They range in size from single-celled yeasts to enormous fungi.

Fungal cells have a cell wall made of a mixture of substances, including chitin. The cytoplasm contains many organelles, including nuclei, ribosomes and mitochondria. Fungi produce digestive enzymes that break down food outside their bodies.

They have saprotrophic nutrition: they feed on dead or decaying organic matter.

Requirements of fungi

  • A moist environment to absorb the soluble products of digestion from their food source.
  • A warm environment so enzymes can work effectively.
  • A nutrient source to provide the raw materials and energy required for growth.
  • They do not require light because they do not rely on photosynthesis.

Reproduction in fungi

Single-celled yeasts usually reproduce asexually by budding, while other fungi reproduce by producing spores.

The mycelium is made up of branching hyphae.

The importance of fungi

  • They are decomposers and play a vital role in nutrient cycles.
  • Fungi may be agents of disease, e.g. athlete’s foot.
  • They may be a source of food.
  • They are used in biotechnology.
1.4

Plants: The plant kingdom

Plants are autotrophs

Plants manufacture food molecules from simple, inorganic substances by photosynthesis, using light.

Plants contain chlorophyll, a light-absorbing pigment, inside cells that have a cellulose cell wall.

The plant kingdom may be divided into three main groups.

Mosses

They cannot grow far away from water.

Ferns

They have roots, stems, complex leaves and vascular tissues, which make them better adapted to life on land. They produce spores for wide dispersal.

They do not have very thick cuticles and often grow in shady, humid areas. The male gametes of ferns must swim through a film of moisture to reach the female gametes for fertilisation.

Angiosperms

Angiosperms are flowering plants. More than 80% of all plant species are angiosperms.

There are two groups of angiosperms:

  • A single cotyledon in the seed: monocotyledons.
  • Two cotyledons in the seed: dicotyledons.

1.7

Protoctists and prokaryotes: Often single celled

Protoctists

Protoctists do not fit into the other main kingdoms. All protoctists have cells with nuclei, and some may have chloroplasts. Many are unicellular, while some are multicellular.

Some feed like animals, others can photosynthesise, and some do both. Algae are classified as protoctists in this classification system.

A unicellular organism with a nucleus is not a prokaryote. It may be a protoctist, but having a nucleus alone does not mean that it must be one.

Bacterial way of life

Bacteria are examples of prokaryotes. They are single-celled organisms that do not have a true nucleus.

They do not have membrane-bound organelles, but they have ribosomes and can carry out life processes without a nucleus.

They may be parasites, feeding on living organisms, or saprotrophs, feeding on dead or decaying organic matter.

Bacteria are so small that they can only be seen using a microscope with sufficient magnification.

1.8

Viruses

Viruses do not show all the typical characteristics of living things. They can only reproduce inside the cells of another living organism.

Some viruses cause disease. They may infect humans, domestic animals or plants.
